# Emotional Experience Arc

Use this only when pacing or player feeling is part of the request. Identify the desired emotion at entry, during sustained play, at a meaningful change, and at resolution. The duration and shape depend on the game: a short arcade round, a long puzzle, and an ongoing social game need different arcs.

Tie emotional shifts to actual agency, challenge, discovery, audiovisual feedback, and recovery after failure. A game may deliberately sustain calm or tension; do not force a fixed escalation/climax pattern. Test whether players experience the intended change instead of inferring it from a document outline.

## Make a creative tradeoff concrete

Translate the promise into a player situation: what the player does, what information or freedom they have, and what consequence matters. Compare proposed treatments against that situation instead of adding features because their names fit the theme.

| Promise | Tempting but conflicting treatment | More fitting direction | Observation still needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calm construction and expression | A countdown pressures every placement | Let experimentation remain reversible; let presentation celebrate the created result | Does the player explore possibilities, or struggle to understand placement? |
| Precise, readable action | A long cinematic effect covers the next landing target | Keep the essential motion readable and make outcome emphasis brief enough for the intended rhythm | Can the player perceive the landing and act again at the designed time? |
| Investigative discovery | A bright objective arrow reveals every inference | Make relevant evidence perceivable while preserving the intended deduction | Can a new player form a useful hypothesis without already knowing the answer? |

These are conditional examples, not bans on timers, cinematics or guidance. The user's confirmed scope can require them. Record the chosen tradeoff in the existing design source, including what it preserves and the unresolved experience hypothesis; do not create a new vision report for a local change.

Use a comparable game's actual player-facing moment to understand the effect of agency, information and presentation. Distinguish learning a method from copying its feature list. For example, [SuperTux's level guidance](https://github.com/SuperTux/supertux/wiki/Level-Design) relates theme consistency and difficulty to playable situations, rather than treating more content as automatically better. Its particular level sizes and pacing are not defaults for another game.
