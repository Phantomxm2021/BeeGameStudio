# Game Economy Design and Tuning

Use only when the game has earnable or spendable resources. Distinguish a score, collectible, crafting input and currency before adding sinks or monetization assumptions. Preserve the game's confirmed resource rules.

## Construct an initial relationship

Start from the intended player decision: spend now, save for a later option, exchange materials or recover from a loss. Identify what the expenditure enables and what the player gives up by choosing it. A resource that only records achievement need not become a currency.

Connect the required resource's creation, storage, transfer/conversion and consumption to actual legal actions. Choose opening stock for the actions available at entry; derive initial rewards and costs from when an option should become available and what competing uses remain. Use the game's relevant unit—turns, encounters, completed activities or elapsed time—rather than assuming continuous income. Proposed availability and effort are design hypotheses, not observed player behavior.

For example, suppose a new design intends a purchase after three completed deliveries, with 5 starting coins and 8 coins awarded per delivery. With no other income or spending, a provisional price of 25 is unavailable at 21 coins after two deliveries and affordable at 29 after three, leaving 4. These illustrative values expose the relationship; they are not default rewards or prices. If deliveries require spending, can fail, or the purchase changes later income, follow those actual actions and changed rates instead of extending the constant-income estimate.

Price alternatives by their useful effect in the intended situation, including limitations, scarcity and the opportunity cost of waiting. Where comparable upgrades exist, a simple baseline can relate cost to benefit; situational options need not collapse to one exchange rate or equal efficiency. If an upgrade changes production or consumption, include that feedback when considering the next choice. This does not require a growth curve, extra currency or sink.

## Revise an existing economy

Retain suitable existing resource relationships and compare acquisition, spending and remaining stock over the affected play window. Include randomness, skill differences and reachable exploit paths when they change the conclusion. Diagnose unusable surplus, unavoidable scarcity, bypassable progression or incentives conflicting with the intended loop; deliberate accumulation is not automatically a defect. Change the smallest responsible source, cost, limit or reward relationship, then observe it through relevant play or simulation. General calculation and evidence limits remain in [parameter analysis](parameter-boundary-model.md).

If paid currency exists, treat fairness, transparency and applicable platform rules as separate product requirements. Do not infer monetization from an ordinary game economy.

Method sources: [Machinations' resource-flow model](https://machinations.io/docs/framework-basics) distinguishes resource creation, storage, conversion, exchange and consumption; [Ian Schreiber's cost-curve discussion](https://gamebalanceconcepts.wordpress.com/2010/07/21/level-3-transitive-mechanics-and-cost-curves/) relates costs and benefits to intended game flow and explains the uncertainty of a new game's initial values. The delivery example above is original illustration. These methods need neither a Machinations account nor a new diagram, spreadsheet or simulator.
